Abuse of Power: Additional Actions

Last updated: October 23, 2026. This page lists additional cases beyond the most egregious abuses of power.


Additional Corrupt and Unethical Activity

Date Administration Action Why is this Unethical? Notes
October 2026 The Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division began presenting grand-jury evidence against Cassidy Hutchinson, the star witness against the president before the House January 6 committee, over whether she lied to Congress. The case was routed to the Civil Rights Division rather than a U.S. attorney’s office, a step a former DOJ official called highly unusual, after a referral from a Republican congressman; department officials themselves reportedly doubt a viable case exists. It fits the pattern of criminal process aimed at the president’s accusers. No charges had been filed as of October 22, 2026.
October 2026 The ambassador to Greece asked a donor to wire $100,000 to her personal credit card (“it won’t show up anywhere”) while forwarding him her lobbying of the IRS commissioner and the Justice Department about his tax and legal problems, and accepted private jets and luxury stays from Greek businessmen whose interests she promoted. Soliciting payment in exchange for official intercession and accepting gifts from parties with business before the embassy violate federal bribery, gratuity and gift rules. The White House asked her to resign. The donor had promised up to $1 million if her intercession succeeded. Lawmakers asked the State Department to investigate.
October 2026 A watchdog sued to force Jared Kushner either out of his role negotiating Iran, Ukraine and Middle East policy or into the ethics rules and financial disclosures that bind federal officers, while he runs a $6 billion investment firm funded almost entirely by foreign governments. Federal law bars officials from acting on matters affecting their financial interests and requires disclosure; Kushner has done neither. His firm holds a stake of more than $1 billion in an Israeli financial group invested in military suppliers, and a U.S.–Russia oil-asset deal he helped broker involves his business partners. Kushner says he is an unpaid volunteer not subject to the rules. See the foreign deals page for the family’s other overseas ventures.
October 2026 The head of the Federal Housing Finance Agency — who generated the mortgage-fraud referrals against Sen. Schiff, Attorney General James and Fed Governor Cook — also pulled the mortgage records of the Deputy Attorney General reportedly to pressure him to bring those cases, then cut his own inspector general’s budget by more than 60%. Using confidential financial records to gain leverage over prosecutors is an abuse of regulatory power, and the Inspector General Act forbids an agency head from obstructing the watchdog’s work; senators called the cut unlawful. The acting inspector general told Congress it ends essentially all criminal investigations. The White House had earlier removed the previous acting inspector general after lawmakers asked him to investigate the referrals (New York Times). The agency head denies reviewing the deputy attorney general’s files.
October 2026 A former FBI supervisor filed a whistleblower complaint alleging that the White House and a top Justice Department official pressed agents to open investigations of anti-ICE protesters whose conduct agents had determined was constitutionally protected — one case prompted by a TikTok video the president watched of protesters explaining their legal rights. Directing federal law enforcement to investigate people for protected speech because the president wants it is retaliation; the complaint says agents were pulled from cartel cases to “make a show for the president,” and that the supervisor was removed from her programs for resisting. The complaint names the then-acting Deputy Attorney General. A senior White House adviser separately urged FBI agents at a counterterrorism conference to bring more aggressive domestic-terrorism cases.
September 2026 The Labor Department’s inspector general found that the former Labor Secretary used government funds for personal travel, mixed personal and official trips (including a stop at a strip club where a security agent was directed to tip a dancer), had employees do personal tasks on duty, drank alcohol on federal property and accepted unreported gifts. Using appropriated funds and federal staff for personal benefit violates federal ethics and appropriations rules; the 44-page report also found she violated the department’s anti-harassment policy, with three dozen witnesses describing a “toxic, intimidating and humiliating” environment (NPR). She resigned in April 2026 before the report was released; her lawyer says she broke no law.
August 2026 The president’s media company began selling early access to his social-media posts — which routinely announce tariffs, military action and policy — for up to $100,000 a month to Wall Street subscribers. Fifty-three former federal prosecutors and agents told a court the scheme likely violates securities, gratuity and conflict-of-interest laws; press-freedom groups sued, arguing that conditioning access to official government announcements on payment to the president’s company violates the First and Fifth Amendments. More than ten financial firms had subscribed within two weeks of launch (NPR).
August 2026 The Pentagon revoked the security clearance of former Air Force Secretary Frank Kendall days after he was quoted in press coverage of the security shortcomings of the Qatari-donated Air Force One, accusing him of disclosing classified information without telling him what he allegedly revealed. Kendall says he disclosed nothing classified and was “completely mystified”; stripping a former official’s clearance for public criticism follows the pattern of clearance revocations used against the president’s critics (Military Times).
August 2026 Prosecutors dropped all charges against four people accused of vandalizing the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool after evidence showed the damage came from a botched renovation by a contractor tied to a Trump donor (USA Today); the president then publicly attacked his own U.S. Attorney for “folding,” demanded she revisit the case, and White House officials asked the Justice Department to consider re-prosecuting the lead defendant. Pressing prosecutors to charge a specific individual after the evidence exonerated him is the direct presidential control of individual prosecutions that Justice Department independence norms exist to prevent (New York Times). The president nominated a Project 2025 co-author as Interior inspector general, the office that would review the renovation and the donor-linked no-bid contracts.
July 2026 Fired FEMA’s chief financial officer in February 2025 over routine payments to New York City migrant shelters and had the Homeland Security Secretary and Elon Musk publicly accuse her of criminal conduct. A federal court held the firing violated due process, and after a court-ordered hearing a magistrate found the officials’ statements “objectively and demonstrably false”. A career senior executive has property and liberty interests that cannot be taken without notice and a hearing; the court rejected the claim that Article II overrides civil-service protections (opinion). The magistrate found the payments had been approved by DOGE staff embedded at DHS, that officials knew their accusations were false, and described Musk’s post of her photo as “little more than state sanctioned intimidation” (order). The government appealed.
July 2026 Court exhibits in the Los Angeles racial-profiling case show that the armed Border Patrol show of force outside Gov. Newsom’s 2025 press conference was, in a federal agent’s sworn deposition, “purely political” with no operational basis; agents’ texts called it “a stunt” and “just a political agenda”. DHS had publicly described the operation as routine enforcement unrelated to the governor. Deploying armed federal agents to intimidate a political opponent’s public event is the use of official power for political ends (NOTUS). Agents were pulled off other operations to stage it.
July 2026 Denied major-disaster declarations to New Jersey, Massachusetts, Rhode Island and New York for February snowstorms after FEMA’s regional offices and NOAA found the states met federal thresholds; New Jersey’s appeal was later denied, costing it about $84 million. An AP analysis found the president has approved disaster requests at the lowest rate of any president since 1989 — about 80% for Republican governors versus 60% for Democrats. Overriding the agencies’ own eligibility findings along partisan lines turns Stafford Act relief into a political reward. The White House denies any politicization.
September 2026 The Wall Street Journal reported that Corey Lewandowski, an unpaid “special government employee” who functioned as the de facto “shadow secretary” at DHS under Kristi Noem, approached senior Qatari and UAE officials with offers to hire him privately to help with matters under DHS authority, including visas, while steering departmental contracting — including a $2.7 billion Palantir contract cut to $1 billion after the company declined a one-on-one meeting with him. Soliciting private payment from foreign governments for help with matters under one’s own agency’s jurisdiction is a direct conflict of interest. The DHS inspector general is investigating how Noem and Lewandowski handled and awarded contracts, and DHS contractors had earlier told White House officials they were asked to pay Lewandowski to protect or expand their contracts. Lewandowski called the report “fiction” and denies seeking foreign contracts or payments from contractors. Senators Blumenthal and Welch demanded records from Secretary Mullin by September 25, 2026; House Democrats had requested the inspector general investigation in March 2026.
August 2026 The Justice Department moved to drop the securities- and wire-fraud case against Indian billionaire Gautam Adani, his nephew Sagar Adani, and an Adani Green Energy executive. On August 11, 2026 the judge granted the dismissal but sharply criticized the department’s handling of it. Judge Nicholas Garaufis wrote that “the irregularities in the decision to dismiss the indictment are concerning,” finding that principal associate deputy attorney general Trent McCotter had “eschewed the professional opinions of innumerable officials from various federal offices” and substituted his own after consulting largely with Adani’s lawyers. The court declined to dismiss the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act bribery and obstruction counts and ordered the department to appear on August 31, 2026 to justify dropping them. The judge then twice rejected the department’s written explanations as adding no new evidence, refusing to let it abandon the bribery and obstruction charges against the remaining defendants. In September 2026 the department fired one of the line prosecutors who had asked to withdraw rather than sign the dismissal; Adani had hired the president’s personal lawyer and pledged a $10 billion U.S. investment (Guardian).
August 2026 FCC Chairman Brendan Carr and Commissioner Olivia Trusty accepted luxury Kennedy Center honors gala tickets from Paramount while the commission was reviewing or about to review Paramount’s multibillion-dollar mergers. Carr has reported such gifts eight times since 2017, worth more than $75,000; Trusty disclosed two 2025 tickets worth more than $12,000. Federal ethics rules bar employees from accepting gifts from entities their agency regulates or that seek official action from it. CREW and the Democracy Defenders Fund filed complaints with the Office of Government Ethics, the FCC inspector general, and the FCC ethics office in August 2026. The FCC says its ethics officials reviewed and approved the gifts under federal ethics rules.
May 2026 The Justice Department opened a criminal investigation touching E. Jean Carroll — who won $83+ million in defamation and sexual-abuse verdicts against Trump personally — examining possible perjury over litigation funding. This aims criminal machinery at the private citizen who won civil judgments against the president — prosecution as personal revenge. The U.S. attorney’s office named in reports publicly denied opening the probe, deepening concerns about its irregularity.
April 2026 A grand jury subpoenaed former senior intelligence and FBI officials in the Justice Department’s criminal probe of ex-CIA Director John Brennan; the career prosecutor handling it, who doubted the case, was removed and an 81-year-old Trump ally installed to run it. This continues the enemies-list prosecutions — the president had named Brennan among those who should be charged — and replacing a skeptical career prosecutor with a loyalist mirrors the pattern courts condemned in the Comey and James cases. Brennan is suing the Justice Department over the probes. By September 2026 the department had widened the inquiry into an alleged conspiracy against the president, subpoenaing former Director of National Intelligence James Clapper among others. The Trump ally brought in to lead it, Joe diGenova, resigned after five months without charges following a tense call with the attorney general, telling a reporter: “If you want indictments where there’s no evidence, you have an ethical problem.” On September 14, 2026 Brennan was subpoenaed to a Florida grand jury in the “grand conspiracy” probe and told he remains a target; former FBI employees from the Mar-a-Lago documents case were subpoenaed as well, and a second Trump-aligned prosecutor left the probe without bringing charges. In October a judge dismissed Brennan’s records suit on standing grounds while calling the alleged preservation failures “damnable.”
December 2025 Pardoned former Honduran president Juan Orlando Hernández, serving 45 years for trafficking tons of cocaine into the U.S. in league with the Sinaloa cartel — announcing it as part of his endorsement of a candidate in Honduras’s election. This is the pardon power deployed as a foreign-election lever, freeing a convicted narco-trafficker to serve the president’s political aims — contradicting the administration’s own drug-war rationale for its lethal boat strikes.
November 2025 After six Democratic lawmakers released a video reminding servicemembers they must refuse illegal orders, the president posted that this was “SEDITION, punishable by DEATH”; the Pentagon then opened an investigation of Sen. Mark Kelly (a retired Navy captain) and said it could recall him to active duty for court-martial. This is threatening military prosecution of a sitting opposition senator for constitutionally protected speech — using the Pentagon as a personal enforcement arm to chill dissent.
November 2025 After newly released Epstein emails mentioned him, the president publicly directed the attorney general to investigate Jeffrey Epstein’s ties to Bill Clinton, Larry Summers, Reid Hoffman, and JPMorgan; the attorney general complied within hours. This is ordering criminal investigations of named political adversaries as deflection; the on-demand compliance illustrates the Justice Department’s loss of independence.
November 2025 Issued blanket federal pardons to 77 people involved in the effort to overturn the 2020 election, including Rudy Giuliani, Mark Meadows, Sidney Powell, John Eastman, and the “fake electors.” This is the use of the pardon power to absolve co-conspirators in a scheme to keep the president himself in power. The pardons are largely symbolic federally — most charges were state-level — underscoring their political-signal purpose.
October 2025 After an FBI raid on his home and office, John Bolton — Trump’s estranged former national security adviser and prominent critic — was indicted on 18 counts of mishandling classified information. The case’s genesis is entangled with retaliation: the president had long threatened to jail Bolton over his book and revoked his security detail, and the raid came amid the administration’s campaign against critics. Unlike the Comey and James cases, courts found no prosecutorial misconduct, and Bolton pleaded guilty to one count in June 2026 — this entry is included with that caveat.
October 2025 Commuted the 87-month fraud sentence of former Rep. George Santos — a vocal loyalist who pleaded guilty to wire fraud and aggravated identity theft — freeing him after roughly three months. This is clemency as a loyalty reward, bypassing Justice Department pardon standards (see the pay-to-play clemency entry on the main page).
October 2025 Demolished the White House East Wing for a roughly $300 million ballroom funded by private donations the president personally solicited from corporations and billionaires. A watchdog analysis found 14 of 27 corporate donors received $50+ billion in new or increased federal contracts within six months, and 16 of 27 faced federal enforcement actions, many of which were suspended. Senators opened an inquiry into “corrupt access.” In August 2026 the Supreme Court let construction proceed 5–4 on standing grounds, expressly not ruling on legality; the Chief Justice’s dissent said the project likely violates federal law. Documents later showed the $333 million in donations included $77 million from anonymous sources and nearly half from federal contractors; the Wall Street Journal reported the president personally tracks which companies have paid into his $800 million-plus fundraising, directing asks of $25–50 million from firms with business before the government.
September 2025 A senior Justice Department official directed U.S. attorney offices in at least seven states to prepare investigations of George Soros’s Open Society Foundations, days after the president publicly called for prosecuting Soros. This is ordering prosecutors to build cases against a designated political enemy rather than following evidence; parallel efforts aimed at stripping tax-exempt status from liberal nonprofits drew IRS-weaponization warnings from tax-law experts.
September 2025 Reporting revealed that hidden FBI cameras had recorded “border czar” Tom Homan in 2024 accepting $50,000 in cash in a takeout bag and agreeing to steer future government contracts. After Trump took office, the Justice Department shut down the investigation. This is weaponization by omission — killing a corruption case against a senior administration ally who was caught on tape; members of Congress demanded the recordings.
September 2025 FBI Director Kash Patel fired agents who had knelt to de-escalate a 2020 protest (conduct internal reviews found proper) and purged agents and analysts who had worked on Trump-related investigations. These are political-loyalty purges of law enforcement. Three senior former officials sued, alleging Patel privately admitted the firings were retaliatory and directed by the White House; the FBI Agents Association said the firings violated the law and agents’ constitutional rights. In October 2026 nine fired employees sued, alleging dismissal without cause and that the director misled Congress about the firings.
August 2025 One week after two days of private meetings between convicted sex-trafficker Ghislaine Maxwell and the Deputy Attorney General (Trump’s former personal defense lawyer), Maxwell was transferred to a minimum-security prison camp — below the security level federal policy requires for sex offenders — where whistleblower documents describe “concierge-style” treatment. This is the appearance of official favors toward a witness with potential information about the president; senators have demanded the still-unexplained transfer records. Maxwell has filed a commutation application with the administration.
August 2025 Revoked Secret Service protection for Kamala Harris, his 2024 opponent. This is the use of discretionary control over protective resources to punish a political rival — part of a pattern of stripping protection and security clearances from critics.
August 2025 Hours after the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported weak job growth, fired BLS Commissioner Erika McEntarfer (confirmed 86–8 by the Senate), claiming without evidence that the numbers were “rigged.” This was retaliation against an independent statistical agency for publishing unwelcome data; the commissioner has no role in producing the estimates. Economists and former BLS heads across party lines called it a dangerous politicization of official statistics.
July 2025 The Justice Department summarily fired Maurene Comey — a veteran federal prosecutor on the Epstein and Maxwell cases and daughter of James Comey — by a one-line email citing “Article II of the Constitution,” without cause or notice. This was retaliation against a career civil servant for her family connection to a critic of the president. A federal judge allowed her wrongful-termination suit to proceed.
July 2025 Paramount paid $16 million toward Trump’s future presidential library to settle his lawsuit over CBS’s editing of a “60 Minutes” interview — a suit legal experts widely called meritless. Three weeks later, the FCC approved Paramount’s $8 billion merger. This is the use of regulatory leverage — FCC merger approval — to extract personal payments from a news organization. Members of Congress opened an investigation into whether the arrangement violated federal anti-bribery statutes. An FCC commissioner and senators likened the payment to a bribe.
July 2025 Threatened to deport Elon Musk, his former aide and a U.S. citizen, and revoke federal subsidies to his companies over political disagreements. This represents an attempt to intimidate his opponents and their supporters.
July 2025 Threatened to revoke citizenship and deport Zohran Mamdani, the Democratic nominee for New York City mayor and a U.S. citizen, over political disagreements. This represents an attempt to intimidate his opponents and their supporters.
June 2025 The president’s sons launched Trump Mobile, a phone service riding on the networks of carriers regulated by, and seeking spectrum approvals from, the Trump administration. This is self-enrichment that puts regulated carriers under implicit pressure to accommodate the president’s family business. The “Made in USA” phone claims were quietly dropped, and promised products hadn’t shipped by 2026, drawing a congressional investigation.
May 2025 The president has not divested from his business dealings nor placed them in a blind trust, as most modern presidents have. Wealthy individuals, including foreign billionaires, and companies have made sizable investments in his company Trump Media and Technology Group, despite it reporting a $400 million loss in 2024. Given the poor performance of the company, it is likely that these investments are being used as a way to curry favor with the president. By mid-2026, the Trump family had realized an estimated $2.3 billion from its crypto ventures alone.
May 2025 The president’s budget called for the largest reduction in NASA’s budget in American history while including an additional $1 billion for investments in Mars exploration. This funding aligns well with Trump ally Elon Musk’s goals to send an unmanned vehicle to Mars in 2026 through his company SpaceX, which often receives grants from NASA.
April 2025 A Milwaukee judge was arrested for allegedly attempting to aid a man in evading arrest by immigration agents. The arrest symbolizes an attempt from the Executive Branch to assert its authority over the co-equal judiciary, especially given that a similar case during Trump’s first term allowed a Massachusetts judge to voluntarily appear before the court and was instead subject to an ethics investigation.
April 2025 The Department of Labor told its staff that they could face termination or criminal charges for speaking to journalists or former employees about agency business. Criminal prosecution for agency leaks is rare, and the move is seen as an attempt to silence dissent and create an atmosphere of fear for whistleblowers.
April 2025 The acting U.S. attorney in DC sent a letter to a respected, peer-reviewed medical journal demanding answers on how its editors handle “competing viewpoints” in the scientific community. Members of the medical community viewed this as a legal threat to align the journal’s content with the political aims of the Trump Administration.
April 2025 Solicited corporate sponsors for the annual White House Easter Egg Roll. Top sponsors included Silicon Valley tech giants like Meta, YouTube, and Amazon. Executives at these companies likewise donated to President Trump’s inauguration fund and were gifted front-row seats. The increased financial contributions from tech giants are notable given the favorable regulatory environment President Trump has promised.
April 2025 President Trump asserted pressure on Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell to lower interest rates by threatening to fire him. The Federal Reserve is designed to be shielded from political pressure. The president is not authorized to fire the Fed Chair because of a disagreement in policy, and threatening his removal is designed to intimidate. Powell served out his term, which ended in May 2026. The pressure campaign escalated into the attempted firing of Fed Governor Lisa Cook, which the Supreme Court ruled unlawful (see the Separation of Powers page).
April 2025 President Trump posted on social media “IT’S A GREAT TIME TO BUY!!! DJT” hours before announcing a 90-day pause on his retaliatory tariffs. In response to the pause, the stock market jumped up. President Trump’s company, Trump Media and Technology Group, which uses “DJT” as a stock symbol, saw its stock price rise 22% after the tariff pause. There is no evidence that this meets the legal definition of insider trading or market manipulation, but questions remain about who had knowledge of the tariff pause before it was public and their investment activities.
March 2025 Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick told a national television audience to “buy Tesla” — “it’ll never be this cheap again” — while his family firm held roughly $840 million in Tesla-linked positions and upgraded the stock to “buy” the same day. Federal ethics rules flatly prohibit officials from using public office to endorse any product or enterprise (5 C.F.R. § 2635.702); ethics experts said the on-air endorsement violated the rule, and senators demanded an ethics investigation.
January 2025 Solicited corporate sponsors for his inauguration, garnering $239 million in donations. The previous record was set at $107 million for the first Trump Inauguration. Presidential inaugurations have never cost nearly that much and it is unknown what the remaining funds are being used for. This represents an unprecedented financial relationship between the Executive Branch and large corporations, raising ethical concerns about government corruption. For example, many tech giants that donated to his inauguration benefited from tariff exemptions for electronics.
